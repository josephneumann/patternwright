# Editorial Lenses

Use these lenses after a blind read and alongside deterministic findings. They are questions, not automatic faults.

## Felt evidence

Name the reader experience first: where attention drifted, trust weakened, emphasis felt forced, emotion arrived pre-explained, or the voice stopped sounding like itself. Point to the exact language that caused the response.

## Semantic lenses

- **False contrast:** Does a negative term create a distinction the sentence does not need? Would the positive claim remain unchanged if the first half disappeared?
- **Significance inflation:** Does the sentence announce importance, novelty, complexity, or emotional weight that its evidence has not earned?
- **Abstraction:** Do nouns such as *impact*, *journey*, *landscape*, or *transformation* hide the actor, action, constraint, or consequence?
- **Register leakage:** Does generic coaching, marketing, academic, therapeutic, or corporate language intrude on the passage's established register?
- **Explanation after evidence:** Does the prose show an image, fact, or reaction and then explain the response the reader has already had?
- **False range:** Are near-synonyms or stacked categories pretending to cover distinct cases?

## Structural and musical lenses

- **Synthetic symmetry:** Are paired clauses or a three-part list clarifying real structure, or making the thought look more complete than it is?
- **Rhythm uniformity:** Do sentences share lengths, openings, internal pivots, or tidy concluding stresses until the prose feels processed?
- **Repeated openings:** Is an anaphora or repeated subject intentional, or merely the default sentence generator?
- **Tidy closer:** Does a paragraph end by summarizing its own lesson instead of landing on an image, fact, implication, or unresolved pressure?
- **Synonym cycling:** Does the prose rename the same person, object, or idea to avoid repetition at the cost of precision?

## Defense and escape conditions

Keep a flagged surface when its contextual function is stronger than the generic risk. Common defenses include:

- an exact technical or legal term;
- quoted or attributed language;
- intentional character, narrator, brand, or genre voice;
- a real logical contrast whose two terms exclude different possibilities;
- repetition that creates memory, pressure, comedy, ritual, or music;
- a list whose members are independently necessary;
- a familiar phrase made specific by surrounding facts.

A defense must describe what the passage gains and what would be lost by removing it. “It sounds fine” is not a defense; neither is a rule match a conviction.

## Revision discipline

Repair causes rather than fingerprints. Restore the hidden actor, event, motive, image, quantity, sequence, or consequence. Remove redundant framing. Break symmetry only when the underlying thought is not symmetrical. Prefer the author's own diction over a generic substitute.

After revision, compare the original and new passage for factual drift, lost voice, flattened rhythm, altered emphasis, and new formulaic surfaces. Retain an original flagged phrase when the proposed cure costs more than the diagnosed problem.

## Policy calibration

Add a deterministic rule only after repeated, independently judged incidents. Give it a narrow expression, a descriptive category, an actionable message, and fixtures for both true and false positives. Keep project-specific bans in a project policy. A warning identifies a place to read; it does not encode the final ruling.
